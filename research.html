Research

My research examines how histories of resource extraction and environmental change shape Indigenous political life, citizenship, and belonging in contemporary India. Working at the intersections of environmental anthropology, political anthropology, and Indigenous Studies, I trace how Adivasi communities navigate state institutions, development, democratic politics, and mobility.

Across these settings, I am interested in the tension between institutional processes that reproduce Indigenous marginalization and the collective practices through which Adivasis make claims, cultivate solidarities, and assert political agency. This concern connects my book on the political afterlives of mining with my emerging research on Indigenous migration and belonging.

Current Book Project

When the Mine Falls Silent

The Afterlives of Extraction in Eastern India

What happens to Indigenous political life when a mine falls silent?

Based on eighteen months of ethnographic research among Ho Adivasis in Jharkhand, the book examines how histories of extraction continue to shape political life after mining operations cease. It centers on Nakahasa, a village where a limestone mine operated intermittently between 1972 and 2012. A proposed expansion into agricultural land and village hamlets prompted a sustained four-year movement against the mine.

Drawing on life histories, mining memories, archival research, bureaucratic encounters, anti-mining mobilization, and elections, I examine how extractive histories become part of the ways people understand themselves, assess others, and make political claims. Caste-inflected bureaucratic practices can position Adivasis as lesser citizens, even as histories of collective resistance provide grounds for solidarity, grassroots leadership, and democratic participation.

The book develops extractive afterlives as a relational and temporal analytic: relationships formed through mining and resistance continue to shape political life after production ends. Paired with the idea of sedimentation, this approach shows how extractive histories acquire durability through their entanglement with older structures of state authority and social difference, without assuming that every subsequent political event is caused by mining.

Emerging Research

Tribal Futures

Indigenous Mobility, Climate Change, and Political Belonging

My second project examines how Indigenous youth negotiate identity, citizenship, and belonging as they migrate from rural eastern India to urban centers amid climate-driven agrarian distress and declining access to forests. Focusing on Ho Adivasi youth moving between Jharkhand and Bangalore, it considers how migration offers livelihoods while exposing young people to precarious work, rising urban heat, and barriers to welfare and recognition.

Through research across rural and urban settings, I examine how migrants use Facebook, YouTube, and WhatsApp to maintain social ties, build networks, forge political solidarities, and sustain and remake Indigenous identities. These questions grew from fieldwork during the COVID-19 lockdown, when digital networks helped migrants coordinate journeys home and seek assistance, and from subsequent research in Bangalore.

By tracing the interconnected geographies of extraction, mobility, and precarity, the project asks how Indigenous political life and claims to belonging are reconstituted across rural and urban spaces.
