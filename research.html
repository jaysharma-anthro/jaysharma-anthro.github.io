Research

My research examines how histories of extraction continue to shape social and political life after mining ends. Drawing on long-term ethnographic research among Ho Adivasi communities in Jharkhand, eastern India, I study how extraction, bureaucracy, social movements, and electoral politics shape everyday relations with land, the state, and one another.

Afterlives of Extraction

Afterlives of Extraction is a book project based on eighteen months of ethnographic fieldwork among Ho Adivasi communities in Jharkhand, eastern India. It asks a simple but overlooked question: what happens after extraction? While scholarship has shown how mining transforms landscapes, labor, and livelihoods, my research examines how extractive histories continue to shape everyday political and social life long after production slows or ceases.

The book argues that extraction leaves behind enduring social and political formations rather than simply abandoned mines or degraded environments. It traces how bureaucratic encounters, electoral politics, development aspirations, and ethical relations continue to be organized through the sedimented histories of extraction. By shifting attention from extraction as an event to its long aftermath, the project offers a new framework for understanding the temporal life of extractive capitalism and its consequences for Indigenous politics and citizenship.

Bureaucracy and Citizenship

Another strand of my research examines the everyday workings of the state through welfare bureaucracy. Rather than understanding bureaucratic indifference as individual apathy or administrative failure, I conceptualize it as a historically sedimented mode of governance that has emerged through long-standing practices of governing Indigenous populations. Drawing on ethnographic research in government offices and villages, I show how bureaucratic routines reproduce exclusion through caste-inflected bureaucratic reasoning and cultural stereotypes, while presenting themselves as neutral and procedural.

This work contributes to the anthropology of the state by demonstrating how bureaucratic authority is sustained through ordinary administrative practices that shape access to welfare, citizenship, and recognition. It also highlights how caste-inflected assumptions and colonial inheritances continue to inform contemporary governance in democratic India.

Democracy, Mobilization, and Political Life

My research also examines how anti-mining mobilization shapes political life beyond moments of protest. Rather than treating social movements solely as sites of resistance, I approach them as spaces where people cultivate political judgment, ethical commitments, and new forms of democratic practice. Through long-term engagement with activists, village leaders, and electoral campaigns, I trace how participation in collective action reshapes relationships to the state, community, and one another.

This work challenges portrayals of Indigenous communities as politically marginal or easily manipulated within electoral politics. Instead, it shows how democratic subjectivities emerge through everyday political practice, producing locally grounded forms of leadership, accountability, and claim-making that continue to shape political life long after organized mobilization has subsided.

Future Research

Building on my work on extraction and Indigenous politics, I am developing a new research project on Ho Adivasi migration and urban labor in contemporary India. The project examines how migration reshapes relationships to land, kinship, work, and political belonging while extending questions of citizenship and state formation beyond the extractive frontier. Together, these projects continue my broader interest in how Indigenous communities negotiate changing political and economic landscapes.
